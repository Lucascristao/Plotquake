# STS-51A - research dossier

Research date: 2026-10-17. Internal historical research for an original Plotquake documentary in US English. This is evidence and editorial guidance, not a finished narration or a rights clearance for all linked media. AGENTS.md was read before preparation. No private thoughts or conversations have been invented.

## The story worth explaining

The central question is how two usable communications satellites, stranded in the wrong orbits, became worth sending people to retrieve - and how the retrieval survived a mechanical problem that prevented the planned berthing method.

The commercial surprise and the engineering story belong together. Insurance losses had changed who had a financial interest in the spacecraft. NASA had a vehicle that could bring large hardware home. The astronauts needed to capture machines that had never been designed for a visitor. The broad achievement was not simply reaching the objects: it was making them safe to handle, securing them for reentry, and preserving enough value for their eventual second lives.

An appropriate promise is **Why NASA Brought Two Satellites Back to Earth**. A more character-led treatment could open with the real For Sale photograph, then ask why that sign belonged in an orbital payload bay. It must explain the money early enough that the audience knows why the rescue exists. It should then earn the outcome through the people and constraints, rather than using the insurance story only as a twist at the end.

The evidence supports an extraordinary salvage operation. It does not support a heist, a rescue of stranded people, a secret mission, satellites falling imminently toward cities, or the claim that NASA spent only $5.5 million on the entire flight.

## A. What failed, and what did not

### A1. Two successful deployments, two failed upper-stage burns

The March 1984 NASA mission report records Westar's deployment from Challenger on February 3 at 20:59 GMT, followed 45 minutes later by the PAM-D ignition. The resulting orbit was approximately 162 by 656 nautical miles, far below the intended operational destination. Palapa's deployment was postponed by two days while the Westar problem was examined. Palapa was released on February 6 at 15:13:16 GMT and reached approximately 148 by 639 nautical miles. The report describes the orbiter's deployment operations as normal in both cases. It still treats the causes as under investigation. [S02, printed p.9/PDF p.12 and mission summary]

This is a useful documented choice: managers did not immediately launch the second satellite without pause. They delayed it, evaluated the first failure, and ultimately proceeded. The material examined here does not establish the complete decision meeting, each participant's position, or a formal narrow vote. Do not manufacture those details.

NASA's 2024 history confirms that both upper stages fired for only a short time and that the Palapa failure repeated the Westar outcome. The same mission also tested the MMU in free flight. Thus the means later used to recover the satellites was successfully demonstrated on the flight that stranded them. [S03]

### A2. Explain the three pieces separately

| Hardware | Function | Consequence for this story |
|---|---|---|
| Space Shuttle Challenger | Carried and released satellites in low Earth orbit | Its successful deployment did not itself deliver them to geostationary orbit. |
| PAM-D upper stage / perigee kick motor | Supplied the next large velocity increase toward the high transfer orbit | This was the failed stage. It is not the backpack or the satellite's later capture fitting. |
| Hughes 376 communications spacecraft | Payload, power systems, communications equipment, its own propulsion hardware | The potentially valuable asset later retrieved by Discovery. |

NASA's fragmentation history identifies the Westar PAM-D as a STAR 48 upper stage. It records nozzle fragmentation shortly after ignition, loss of a sustainable burn, and subsequent separation of the PAM-D from Westar. The adjacent Palapa entry likewise records separation of its PAM-D. Therefore **the failed upper stages were not the objects brought home**. [S04, Westar entry printed p.250; Palapa entry immediately before it]

The stinger entered the **apogee kick motor (AKM) nozzle of the satellite**, as named in the STS-51A report. Do not illustrate an astronaut docking with the detached, damaged PAM-D. The archival literature sometimes calls multiple things a rocket engine, so a simple labeled graphic is more useful than repeating acronyms in narration. [S15, printed pp.8-9]

A NASA engineer's dedicated technical paper supplies useful details for that graphic: the astronaut inserted the stinger probe through the AKM nozzle throat into the empty motor case; a lever released three spring-loaded fingers for initial capture, then a threaded retraction mechanism clamped the assembly. The rest of the device could later be detached while its probe remained inside the motor through landing. Consequently, removing the stinger need not mean extracting every component from inside the nozzle. Do not say that the satellite's AKM had never fired; the preflight press-kit terminology described its case as spent. [S29; press-kit indexed excerpt, not a fully inspected PDF]

For Westar, the contemporary litigation record says a burn planned for 85 seconds ended after its STAR 48 exit cone disintegrated about four seconds after ignition. NASA's fragmentation catalog uses the broader wording within ten seconds. Use a few seconds if the exact count does not serve the scene. The court also documents that Western Union had considered Ariane and switched back to the Shuttle on its assessment of pricing and reliability. This was an economic choice made before the outcome was known. [S05]

The spacecraft were not inert wrecks. Their remaining propulsion and communication capabilities were necessary to prepare them for rendezvous. Operationally useless in these orbits is different from physically destroyed.

### A3. The geography must remain intelligible

Geostationary communications operations require a much higher, equatorial orbit, roughly 22,300 miles above Earth. Discovery was a low Earth orbit vehicle; it could not simply follow a satellite to that operational altitude. What made this retrieval possible was the satellites' unfortunate accessibility in lower orbits, combined with advance maneuvering. Do not show Discovery flying out to geostationary orbit. The initial elliptical orbits, the later adjusted orbits and the capture altitude are different stages; do not mix a figure from one with another. [S03; S13]

## B. Why the insurers and NASA wanted to attempt it

### B1. A primary agreement, not an invented bounty

NASA's Space News Roundup of August 31, 1984 reports that an agreement was signed on August 13 for Palapa B2's retrieval. The named organizations were **Merrett Syndicates Ltd.** and **International Technology Underwriters**. They represented the majority of domestic and foreign underwriters interested in the return. NASA was to be reimbursed for preparing and executing the retrieval, **not exceeding $4.8 million**. The article says Hauck's crew had trained for the possibility for much of the summer. It identifies the already planned outbound Telesat-H and Syncom IV payloads. [S06, first page]

This agreement is the best authentic document for the money section. It also prevents the impression that NASA launched an otherwise empty shuttle solely as a tow truck. STS-51A carried two new satellites and other work as well as its retrieval equipment.

NASA's wakeup-call chronology records a final figure of **$5.5 million to NASA for both retrievals**, following the addition of Westar. It associates the November 14 wakeup music, the theme from For a Few Dollars More, with CAPCOM Bob Springer's explanation of the financial arrangement. This supports the historical anecdote; it does not clear the music for reuse. [S07, printed p.16]

### B2. Financial ledger and limits

| Figure | What the source actually identifies | Use in narration |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $4.8 million | August 13 agreement: NASA costs for preparing and executing Palapa retrieval | Confirmed contemporary NASA announcement. |
| $5.5 million | Payment to NASA for retrieving both satellites | Call it the retrieval payment, not total mission cost or astronaut pay. |
| Approximately $180 million | Combined insurance payments to the original owners, in NASA's historical chronology | An insurance payout, not the price tag of two bare satellites. |
| $5 million | Hughes technical assistance for retrieval, as reported in the NASA chronology | A separate payment; not part of the $5.5 million NASA figure. |
| $30-40 million Palapa; $30 million Westar | Stephen Merrett's reported resale expectations immediately after the flight | Forecasts, not verified completed sale proceeds. |

The last three rows come from NASA's Astronautics and Aeronautics chronology for November 8-16, 1984, which cites contemporary reporting. It identifies the owners as Indonesia for Palapa and Western Union for Westar. These are valuable documented contemporary figures, but this compilation is not the underlying contracts, insurers' settlement statements, or final resale accounts. [S08, November 1984, around printed pp.515-516]

Do not subtract two numbers and announce a profit. The sources do not close the full ledger of retrieval, refurbishment, storage, relaunch, ownership interests and actual sales. A successful physical recovery and an insurer recouping part of a loss are defensible. A proven profitable business for all parties is not established here.

### B3. People behind the financial decision

Commander Rick Hauck later identified **Stephen Merrett** and **Jim Barrett** as leading insurance participants. He remembered Merrett emphasizing that the crew should not risk their lives merely to recover the property. Hauck described the challenge principally as uncertainty of mission success. He also recalled objecting to a NASA public statement that made the task sound routine. A documented brief quotation usable with attribution is **“we were close to getting neither of them back.”** This is a 2003 recollection, not recorded prelaunch dialogue. [S09, printed pp.30-37]

Lloyd's is an insurance market with participating underwriters, not simply one ordinary insurance company owning all of the risk. Its modern institutional article confirms market involvement in funding the rescue, but has errors in the chronology and mechanics; see the discrepancy notes. [S10]

The contract changes the incentives without requiring a villain. The machines still existed; insurance payments did not make them physically disappear. Salvage rights and the prospect of sale gave participants a reason to pay for recovery. Explain the transaction as the documented arrangement, not a claim that maritime salvage law automatically transfers ownership of every object found in space.

## C. The crew and the mission they were actually flying

| Person | Specific function in the recovery | What the audience should be able to follow |
|---|---|---|
| Frederick H. Rick Hauck, commander | Piloted Discovery through proximity operations and coordinated the crew | Kept the shuttle close enough to work, without treating capture as routine. |
| David M. Dave Walker, pilot | Helped with EVA preparation and served as the intravehicular coordinator | Work inside the ship mattered as much as the astronaut visible outside. |
| Joseph P. Joe Allen | First MMU capture, Palapa; manual handling in both recoveries | One astronaut stabilizing a large object while another fitted its adapter. |
| Dale A. Gardner | Worked Palapa's berthing hardware; flew the MMU to capture Westar | His adapter work was essential after the primary mounting method failed. |
| Anna L. Fisher | Operated the Remote Manipulator System / Canadarm and supported rendezvous | She moved the astronaut-and-satellite combination and, later, Allen's work platform. |

Crew names, launch and landing are verified by NASA's mission record. STS-51A was Discovery's second flight and the fourteenth Shuttle mission. It launched November 8, 1984 from Kennedy's Pad 39A and landed at Kennedy November 16; mission duration was 7 days, 23 hours, 44 minutes, 56 seconds. [S01]

NASA's 2024 history records that the crew was originally assigned in November 1983 to STS-41H. Scheduling and payload changes moved the assignment to STS-51A. The eventual outbound payloads were **Anik D2** for Canada and **Leasat 1 / Syncom IV-1** for the US Navy. Allen deployed Anik on flight day 2; Gardner deployed Leasat on day 3. The recovery tasks came afterward. The history also records a one-day launch delay for upper-level winds. [S17]

Fisher's first flight and her distinction as the first mother in space can supply human scale, but the story should introduce her through responsibility. In her 2011 oral history she describes the strain of doing rendezvous while two astronauts suited up and Walker helped them. She remembers simultaneous motion of Earth, shuttle, satellite and arm requiring intense concentration. She also says the crew had worried before launch about fitting the bridge structure because the actual spacecraft were already in orbit and unavailable for a fit check. [S11, printed pp.31-34]

Do not reduce Fisher to the person watching the men work. Her control of Canadarm placed them and the hardware where the procedure required. Do not merge the similar-looking images of Gardner and Allen or swap which man captured which satellite.

## D. Preparing a capture without handles

These spacecraft had not been designed for astronaut retrieval. The tools had to make an existing feature into an interface. A capture and a secure return were separate problems: first stop relative motion and hold the object; then transfer it, fit its berthing adapter, and lock it into its payload-bay supports.

Allen's 2004 recollection explains the **stinger** as a manually operated device with an expanding mechanism inside the rocket opening and a hand-operated clamp. He says automatic trigger difficulties on the earlier Solar Max mission influenced that preference. The device was attached in front of the suited MMU pilot. It was not a spear fired across a gap or a gun launching a tether. His account also names Barrett and Merrett and distinguishes retrieving property from the earlier Solar Max repair in orbit. [S12, printed pp.4-6]

The MMU used **24 gaseous-nitrogen thrusters** and separate hand controls for translation and rotation. A technical paper coauthored by Bruce McCandless and NASA's Charles Whitsett describes redundant electronics, electrical and propulsion subsystems. This is a controlled maneuvering backpack, not a continuously burning movie rocket. Use cold-gas movement and the real archival pace. [S14]

The NASA rendezvous history describes the exceptionally involved orbital plan: two outbound deployments, two inbound recoveries, combined proximity and EVA activities, and **44 orbital-change maneuvers** within a tight propellant budget. Both targets were maneuvered before Discovery launched to make the two encounters compatible. [S13, printed p.127]

Flight director Randy Stone's oral history adds a less visible constraint: after the first spacecraft was in the bay, the attitudes needed to chase the second could expose it to temperatures affecting the hydrazine left aboard. He recalls detailed work to avoid freezing, leaks and a subsequent ground hazard. Treat this as **a hazard they analyzed**, not a leak that happened. His recollection of who led which shift is qualified in the interview and should not override the flight records. [S16, printed p.19]

Hauck's memory of rehearsing a very unlikely astronaut/MMU departure from the shuttle is evidence that loss-of-control scenarios were considered. Do not turn his discussion of a reaction time in that hypothetical into an actual fifteen-second countdown during the flight. This restraint makes the real tension more credible.

## E. Palapa: the capture worked, the planned mount did not

### E1. November 12, flight day 5

The capture involved Allen in the MMU with Gardner outside assisting and Fisher controlling Canadarm. The astronaut's stabilizing system stopped Palapa's rotation after the stinger locked into the AKM. The arm then brought the combination into working position. The part that failed was the **antenna bridge structure (ABS), also called the A-frame**, intended to provide the next secure grapple/mounting interface.

The December mission report gives a specific cause: a **waveguide assembly projected farther outward than expected**, obstructing the common bracket clamps. Calling it the wrong diameter of the entire satellite would overstate the evidence. Neither the MMU nor the stinger had failed. [S15, printed pp.8-9]

The report says Allen left and stowed the MMU, stood in a portable foot restraint beside the pallet, and held the satellite while Gardner removed the capture device, covered the AKM nozzle and installed the adapter on the bottom of the spacecraft. They manually berthed and latched it. This was a replacement method for controlling the satellite while the real return hardware was fitted, not simply an astronaut tossing a weightless object into an open bay. [S15]

For the basic scene geography, NASA's 2019 historical narrative confirms Allen's MMU capture, Fisher's grapple and the subsequent manually controlled berthing. The first EVA lasted about six hours. [S18]

### E2. Training, improvisation, and what the evidence actually says

There is a meaningful difference among the accounts:

- The December 1984 report calls the manual recovery a **“practiced backup procedure.”** [S15]
- Fisher says the crew had discussed manual recovery with flight director Milt Heflin before launch because the fit could not be checked on the actual satellite. [S11]
- Allen recalls Walker's written backup note as **“Improvise.”** His retrospective account describes devising the specific hand-holding arrangement during the problem. [S12]

The defensible synthesis is that **they adapted a manual-recovery alternative considered before launch**. This preserves the training as well as the choices made in orbit. It avoids depicting an entirely rehearsed, effortless switch or a plan invented from nothing under an imminent life-or-death deadline.

Allen says he received too much credit for holding the satellite, whereas Gardner performed difficult adapter work by himself. This is a useful editorial correction: the dramatic image of the man holding the object should lead to what the less conspicuous partner was doing. A voiceover should not make Allen a lone savior. His account of glare, hands feeling warmth and cold, and limited tactile feedback can guide visual and sound choices, but direct dialogue must come from real recordings or be explicitly attributed as retrospective recollection.

### E3. Mass is not weight

In orbit a hand can support an apparently huge spacecraft without supporting its Earth weight against gravity, but the object's mass and inertia remain. It must be started, stopped and prevented from touching delicate surfaces or nearby structure. The retrieved weights in NASA payload statistics are roughly **1,262 lb Palapa and 1,119 lb Westar**; other sources use round numbers or values corresponding to different configurations. Avoid an inflated 11,000- or 12,200-pound satellite claim found in online summaries. If no exact mass is needed, a satellite larger than a person communicates the task without a dubious figure. [S19]

## F. Westar: a changed method, not the same cliffhanger twice

November 13 allowed suit servicing and planning. The crew did not merely hope the same bridge would fit the next object. The report says they decided to **omit the A-frame** because Westar might have the same waveguide configuration. The main change was putting Allen in the **Manipulator Foot Restraint at the end of Canadarm**, rather than the portable foot restraint fixed beside a pallet. That gave Fisher a better way to position him to handle the satellite, reducing workload and the potential for solar-cell damage. [S15]

On November 14 Gardner made the MMU capture and stopped Westar's spin; Allen received and steadied it while its adapter was fitted. NASA's 2019 history gives the second EVA as 5 hours 42 minutes. The contemporary mission report uses 5 hours 43 minutes with hatch times of 11:08-16:51 GMT; use **nearly six hours**, or use 5:42 with that historical source, rather than suggesting the task took half the first EVA's time. [S18; S15]

Keep the second operation's spatial logic clear: **Gardner guided the captured satellite toward Allen, while Fisher positioned Allen's work platform on the arm**. Do not suggest the same arm grappled the MMU/satellite assembly while simultaneously carrying Allen on its end. This differs from the primary Palapa configuration and is exactly why showing a changed method matters.

The story can now change rhythm. Palapa demonstrates the obstruction and the consequences of losing the planned support. Westar demonstrates learning: a different work platform and sequence make the second recovery more controlled. Repeating the same mechanical surprise would be factually wrong and narratively weaker.

Both backpacks and suits operated without major anomalies during the two retrieval EVAs. There were other minor mission issues, including lights and a display, but do not borrow them as if they disabled the recovery at its most dramatic point. None of the evidence consulted supports a runaway satellite, loss of an astronaut, an oxygen emergency or an uncommanded collision during capture.

## G. Home, sale, and second lives

The famous **For Sale** photograph is a real mission image: Gardner and Allen posed with the sign after the two objects were secured. NASA's gallery captions distinguish specific November 12 and November 14 images. Use the real caption and identity of the subject. Do not call the photograph a contemporaneous advertisement showing a verified asking price. [S20]

On **December 7, 1984**, the crew received Lloyd's silver medals at the White House. A preserved presidential speech draft names **all five astronauts**, dates the event, and frames the achievement as preservation of commercial property. This is stronger evidence than the errors in the modern Lloyd's webpage. It is a prepared remarks document; do not claim its every word was actually spoken without checking event video/audio. [S21]

### G1. Westar became AsiaSat 1

AsiaSat's own history says it bought the spacecraft and contracted Hughes to refurbish it and adapt its payload for Asian requirements. It launched on a **Long March 3 from Xichang on April 7, 1990**, entering service the following month. This was not just an object recovered and stored: the original machine became infrastructure for another region. [S22]

The company's milestone page identifies Myanmar TV service beginning in May 1990 and the satellite's use for the 1990 Asian Games. These provide concrete consequences that are more illuminating than stopping at a spacewalk medal. [S23]

AsiaSat's account reports retirement in February 2003 after thirteen years of service. Keep the corporate provenance clear and avoid turning promotional superlatives into independent judgments. It incorrectly says the recovery occurred one year after the 1984 launch; use the NASA dates for that interval. [S22]

### G2. Palapa returned to Indonesia

NASA's historical account identifies the relaunch as **Palapa B2R on April 13, 1990, aboard a Delta**, after which title returned to Indonesia. [S17]

The Aeronautics and Space Report launch table independently lists Palapa-B2R on that date. The same table lists **Palapa B-2P on March 20, 1987**, demonstrating these are different launches. Do not repeat the 2010 servicing study's erroneous label B2P for the recovered 1990 vehicle. [S24]

The exact final purchase prices and net amounts recovered by each insurer have not been verified from completed-sale records in this research. No such figures are necessary to show that both objects returned to useful operation.

## H. How far the conclusion can go

STS-51A demonstrated the capacity to retrieve commercial spacecraft that had not been designed for retrieval. It was also the **last operational flight of the MMU**, not the end of all satellite rescue. NASA's MMU history explains that later decisions about the device reflected operational needs and risk assessments; its retirement cannot be reduced to a single failed performance on this successful mission. [S25]

In August 1986 Reagan announced a different division between NASA and private launch providers. His statement said NASA would leave the business of launching private satellites and devote scarce resources to other functions. This is a documented policy change after Challenger. It does not establish that STS-51A's salvage failed financially, or that the accident directly outlawed repair and retrieval. [S26]

There is a decisive counterexample to a simplistic ending: **STS-49 in May 1992 recovered Intelsat VI in low orbit, attached a new motor and released it to reach operational orbit**. The Shuttle also continued scientific servicing. Therefore never say no one rescued a commercial satellite again after 1984, that all servicing stopped after Challenger, or that jetpacks and salvage were the same policy. [S27]

NASA's 2010 servicing study places Palapa/Westar among the precedents for commercial servicing, while distinguishing technical possibility from a business case. It discusses comparison with replacement cost, risks, revenue and mission value. A careful ending can say the ability survived in different forms while routine return-and-resale did not become the universal operating model. Do not declare a single cause for that outcome or imply this document proves the economics of every later case. [S28]

The best ending returns to the premise: these were functioning assets with no useful route to their intended jobs. Human operation and purpose-built interfaces gave them another route. The evidence of success is not only the sign or the medal; it is the later service delivered by the returned hardware.

## J. Research-backed narrative material for an original 2,500-3,000-word script

The following is an optional allocation of explanatory work, not a fixed act template or a duration gate:

1. **The photograph and the question.** What is for sale in this payload bay? Establish that the objects were going home and that insurers had already paid for their loss. Introduce their value as usable hardware, rather than an unqualified giant dollar graphic.
2. **Why working hardware could be useless.** Follow one deployment, distinguish the Shuttle from the PAM-D, show the intended orbital job, and explain why another burn did not simply solve everything. Include the pause before Palapa and the second failure without inventing the managers' debate.
3. **An unusual customer meets an unusual vehicle.** Use the authentic August agreement, explain salvage incentives and separate the payment from the full mission bill. Introduce the crew through the work each person must do.
4. **Making the targets retrievable.** Move from insurance paperwork to the problem of machines without handles. Demonstrate stinger, MMU, Canadarm and berthing adapter as different interfaces. Add the orbital and thermal planning only when needed to understand a choice.
5. **Palapa and the blocked clamp.** Show what worked before what failed. Let the audience see why a small protrusion changes the whole handling method. Keep the backup-plan nuance. Give Gardner's adapter work and Fisher's placement enough time to make the manual solution understandable.
6. **Westar and the revision.** Show changed body position and workflow. The tension shifts from an unfamiliar solution to applying knowledge while keeping another valuable object safe.
7. **The proof after landing.** Let the real sign supply relief, then show the return to useful service in 1990. AsiaSat's broadcasting and Palapa's return are the consequence of recovering actual hardware.
8. **A measured legacy.** Distinguish a demonstrated capability from the routine business NASA had once imagined. Include a brief 1986 policy context only if it strengthens the closing question. Do not turn the last minutes into a broad Challenger documentary.

Possible human details, only if individually useful: Fisher's demanding arm operation amid competing visual motion; Allen's insistence that Gardner deserved more credit; Hauck's objection to making the recovery sound easy; engineers' inability to fit-check the bridge on spacecraft already in orbit. These carry the theme without fabricated private motives.
